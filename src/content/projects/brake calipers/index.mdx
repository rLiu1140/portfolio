### Overview
As a term-long project for ME 240: Intro to Mechanical Design and Manufacturing, teams of five were tasked with developing rear bike brake calipers. Within my team, I was entirely responsible for the design process, from initial sketches and CAD through FEA-driven optimization, testing-backed iteration, and technical documentation.

### Requirements
The design was driven by hard requirements specified by ISO 4210 bicycle safety standards as well as team-defined goals.

| Metric | Ideal | Marginal |
|---|---|---|
| Stopping distance (25 km/h) | ≤ 7 m | ≤ 15 m |
| Mass | ≤ 200 g | ≤ 400 g |
| Material | Nylon-12 (required) | — |
| Appearance | 5/5 | 2/5 |

### Concept Development
The design space was constrained by fixed caliper and brake pad mounting holes, a predetermined lever arm length, and clearance for wheel passage. A rough boundary of this space was hand-sketched before modeling it in CAD. This allowed for finite element analysis (FEA) without over-constraining any load paths.

#### Calculations
The relationship between brake geometry and stopping performance was derived using kinematics and a moment balance equation. This showed that the input force required for a given stopping distance is inversely proportional to the input and cable lever arm lengths.

$$
F_{in} = \frac{mv_i^2\,r_{out}\,r_{pad}\,r_{tire}}
{2\mu d\,r_{in}\,r_{cable}\,r_{brake}}
$$

#### Load Path Visualization
Running FEA on the initial design-space model revealed critical load paths through the part. Loads closely followed the inner radius near the wheel, concentrating at the caliper mounting hole and producing the largest displacement at the brake pad mounting hole. These simulations helped identify where material was structurally necessary and where it could be removed to minimize mass.

#### Topology Optimization
The profile of each caliper was created using splines to precisely outline the outer load path. Once the boundaries were defined, ribs were sketched and created on each side of the calipers to reflect the direction and magnitude of the internal load paths. The resulting cross-section resembled an I-beam, maximizing stiffness and reducing deflection through an increased moment of inertia. Fillets were then sized based on local stress, with large internal fillets around the stress-concentrated caliper mounting hole and smaller external fillets distributed around less critical regions.

FEA was used iteratively throughout the design process: first to visualize load paths, and then to verify the modeled geometry against stress and deflection requirements before committing to manufacturing.

### Design Iterations
#### V1

Initial real-world testing passed all performance metrics, confirming that the FEA results were generally predictive. However, the cable mounting hardware introduced an unaccounted-for bending moment on the right caliper. Removing an extra mounting nut allowed the cable to sit flush against the caliper face, reducing stopping distance from 254 in to 187 in and lever lockout force from 150 N to 102 N.

#### V2

#### Changes from V1
- Shifted the right caliper cable mounting face to be coplanar with the left caliper, eliminating the bending component.
- Biased material placement along the new diagonal load path created by the offset face.
- Redesigned the right caliper arm rib from an I to an X to increase bidirectional rigidity.
- Tightened bolt hole tolerances (6.40 mm → 6.27 mm) to reduce mounting wobble.

| Metric | Target (Marginal / Ideal) | V1 | V2 | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stopping distance (25 km/h) | ≤15 / ≤7 m | 13.5 m | 11.5 m | -14.8% |
| Mass | ≤400 / ≤200 g | 30.2 g | 31.5 g | +4.3% |

The updated design reduced stopping distance by 2 meters, a 14.8% improvement in braking performance. This came at the cost of only 1.3 g of additional mass, which is negligible relative to the bike and rider system and makes the tradeoff worthwhile.

### Reflection
Without the provided material constraint, exploring metal alloys and more organic geometries could further improve performance without significantly increasing weight through the same SLS 3D-printing process.

Overall, the design process was relatively standard but comprehensive, while the project highlighted the importance of real-world testing alongside simulation.
