### Overview
As a term-long project for ME 240: Intro to Mechanical Design and Manufacturing, teams of five were tasked with developing rear bike brake calipers. Within my team, I was entirely responsible for the design process, from initial sketches and CAD through FEA-driven optimization, testing-backed iteration, and technical documentation.

<Image src={brakeAssembly} alt="Brake Assembly" style="border-radius: 6px; max-width: 75%; height: auto;" />

### Requirements
The design was driven by hard requirements from ISO 4210 bicycle safety standards, alongside goals the team defined internally.

| Metric | Ideal | Marginal |
|---|---|---|
| Stopping distance (25 km/h) | ≤ 7 m | ≤ 15 m |
| Mass | ≤ 200 g | ≤ 400 g |
| Material | Nylon-12 (required) | — |
| Appearance | 5/5 | 2/5 |

### Concept Development
The design space was bounded by fixed caliper and brake-pad mounting holes, a predetermined lever arm length, and clearance for wheel passage.

<Image src={brakeDrawing} alt="Brake Drawing" style="border-radius: 6px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;" />

A rough sketch of this space was hand-drawn before modeling it in CAD, which allowed for finite element analysis (FEA) without over-constraining any load paths.

#### Calculations
The relationship between brake geometry and stopping performance was derived from kinematics and a moment balance equation, showing that the input force required for a given stopping distance is inversely proportional to the input and cable lever arm lengths.

$$
F_{in} = \frac{mv_i^2\,r_{out}\,r_{pad}\,r_{tire}}
{2\mu d\,r_{in}\,r_{cable}\,r_{brake}}
$$

#### Load Path Visualization
Running FEA on the initial design-space model revealed critical load paths through the part: loads followed the inner radius near the wheel closely, concentrated at the caliper mounting hole, and produced the largest displacement at the brake pad mounting hole. These results identified where material was structurally necessary and where it could be safely removed to cut mass.

### Design Iterations
#### V1
Initial real-world testing passed all performance metrics, confirming that the FEA results were generally predictive. However, the cable mounting hardware introduced an unaccounted-for bending moment on the right caliper.

<Image src={brakev1Test} alt="Brake v2 Test" style="border-radius: 6px; max-width: 50%; height: auto;" />

Removing an extra mounting nut let the cable sit flush against the caliper face, reducing stopping distance from 6.45 m to 4.75 m and lever lockout force from 150 N to 102 N.

- Shifted the right caliper's cable mounting face to be coplanar with the left caliper, eliminating the bending component.
- Biased material placement along the new diagonal load path created by the offset face.
- Redesigned the right caliper arm rib from an I to an X for greater bidirectional rigidity.
- Tightened bolt hole tolerances (6.40 mm → 6.27 mm) to reduce mounting wobble.

| Metric | Target (Marginal / Ideal) | V1 | V2 | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stopping distance (25 km/h) | ≤15 / ≤7 m | 13.5 m | 11.5 m | -14.8% |
| Mass | ≤400 / ≤200 g | 30.2 g | 31.5 g | +4.3% |

The updated design reduced stopping distance by 2 meters (a 14.8% improvement in braking performance) at the cost of only 1.3 g of additional mass, which is negligible relative to the bike-and-rider system and well worth the tradeoff.

### Reflection
Without the provided material constraint, exploring metal alloys and more organic geometries could further improve performance without adding significant weight, using the same SLS 3D-printing process.

Overall, the design process was straightforward yet comprehensive, and the project highlighted the importance of real-world testing alongside simulation.
