### Overview
As FRC teams shifted to swerve drive, Team 610 faced significant limitations with its traditional tank-drive architecture. Tank drive restricted independent rotation and translation, which limited vision-based navigation and made maneuvers predictable during defense. However, implementing swerve required substantial software development, ruling out an in-season transition.

To support Team 610's adoption of swerve drive, I independently designed, machined, and assembled a scaled-down drivetrain with all the components of a standard competition drivetrain.

### Design
The chassis measured 21 × 21 in, compared to the 30 × 30 in footprint of typical competition robots. This smaller size provided enough space for electronics while keeping the drivetrain compact and stable. Chassis tubing was machined on a manual mill, while gussets were CNC-routed.

Four corner-mounted MK4i swerve modules powered by Falcon 500 motors drove the chassis. The inverted motor configuration protected the motors during collisions and lowered the center of mass. Colson wheel compatibility provided durability for repeated testing.

Instead of conventional wood-and-foam bumpers, curved ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) plates were riveted around the chassis corners. These plates were selected for their high impact resistance and ductility and offered a lightweight, simple way to protect the swerve modules from accidental impact.

An adjustable-angle camera mount at the front of the drivetrain allowed programmers to optimize camera position for AprilTag and reflective-tape detection.

### Electronics
Standard FRC components were used throughout to ensure software developed on the drivetrain would accurately translate to the competition robot.

A 12 V, 18 Ah SLA battery powered the drivetrain through a power distribution hub. Eight 40 A, 12 V brushless motors drove the swerve modules, with an absolute encoder tracking the steering angle of each module alongside an IMU for robot heading and acceleration. All devices were connected to a common onboard CAN network. An OM5P router provided the network connection needed for wireless driver-station control, and Wago distribution hubs were placed throughout the chassis to simplify wiring and serviceability.

### Results
The drivetrain allowed programmers to develop both teleoperated and autonomous vision-assisted swerve control. Swerve drive was successfully implemented in the 2024 season, with progressively more advanced automation introduced each year:

- **2024**: Vision-assisted shooting and automated drive-to-target
- **2025**: Fully automated multi-level pick-and-place
- **2026**: Fully automated dual-turret shooting while moving

The transition to swerve drive was fundamental to Team 610's recent success, helping advance the team's ranking from the top 250 in 2023 to the top 100 in 2025 and 2026, out of 3,700+ teams worldwide. It also directly contributed to the team earning the Innovation in Control and Autonomous Awards.
